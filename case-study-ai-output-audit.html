AI output audit for an OSINT threat-intelligence platform

Evaluating an in-house AI module’s threat scores, red flags and narrative summaries, and separating model-output failures from functional software bugs.

Role
AI model evaluation, functional bug testing and product improvement input
Client type
In-house product (Kainotomia Pte Ltd)
Tools
Google Sheets, Gemini
Outcome
Findings tracker used by the engineer to refine the AI engine’s RAG pipeline for customer readiness, alongside separate functional bug reporting and feature input

01Context

My company, Kainotomia Pte Ltd, develops an OSINT (open-source intelligence) threat-intelligence platform. The platform collects from publicly accessible sources: public channels, public groups and public stories. It includes a new, in-house-trained AI module that produces threat scores (0–100), red flags and narrative summaries.

My employer gave permission to describe this evaluation work, but not to show the platform or any real records.

02My role

My role covered three areas across the platform: evaluating the AI module’s outputs and logging failures so the engineer could refine the AI engine’s RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) pipeline; testing the platform for functional software bugs and reporting them to the engineering team; and contributing my own input on how to improve the system’s functionality. I built the AI-inconsistency findings tracker described below on my own with Gemini. I did not design, train or build the underlying AI model itself.

03Approach

Step 1

Auditing scores, flags and quote context

I review the generated threat scores (0–100), red flags and narrative summaries against the raw public posts they cite. I check for hallucinations, logic failures, and deterministic extraction or NLP errors. In particular, I verify whether a cited quote reflects personal intent or is actually third-party news, sarcasm or gaming talk, and whether the evidence contradicts flags the system marked as positive.

Decision

Checking each cited quote in its full thread catches false positives where the model treated news reposts, sarcasm or gaming slang as literal personal intent. [VERIFY]

Step 2

Checking demographic sources and cross-case links

I verify whether demographic attributions (language, timezone and location) come from the raw content itself or from gazetteer heuristics. I also inspect cross-case links to confirm whether two profiles have a genuine connection, or were linked only because of shared public channels, moderation bots or self-matching.

Decision

Separating raw-content evidence from heuristic defaults prevents gazetteer guesses and shared-channel bots from looking like verified intelligence links. [VERIFY]

Step 3

Tracking AI inconsistencies separately from functional bugs

I built an AI-inconsistency findings tracker in Google Sheets on my own, with Gemini, so the engineer could review specific failure cases and refine the RAG of the AI engine for customer use. Its columns capture the finding title, assessment, verdict, profile reference and evidence. I also tested the platform for functional software bugs, reported them separately to the engineering team, and contributed input on how to improve system functionality.

Trade-off

Maintaining two logs requires classifying every defect before logging it, but it keeps RAG and training-data feedback clean of routine software bugs. [VERIFY]

05Outcome

The findings tracker gave the engineer a structured log of AI-output failures to review and use when refining the RAG of the AI engine so that outputs would be accurate when customers used the system.

Alongside the AI audit, I tested and reported functional software bugs to the engineering team in a separate log and contributed recommendations on how to improve the platform’s functionality.

06What I learned

I can identify where the model’s outputs fail. I cannot yet explain why the underlying statistical model produced them. Closing that gap is one reason I want formal training. [VERIFY]
